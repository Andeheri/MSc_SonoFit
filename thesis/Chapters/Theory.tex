\section{Pulsed-Wave Doppler}
\label{sec:theory_pw_doppler}

In the present system, \gls{PW Doppler} is used to measure the blood flow velocity. It works by emitting a series of high frequency sound pulses into the tissue at a constant \gls{PRF}. Each pulse consists of a burst of sound waves with a pulse center frequency $f_c$ with $N_{cycles}$ cycles. The pulse length $T$ is then given by $T = N_{cycles} / f_c$. After a pulse is emitted, the system records the returning echoes. This is commonly referred to as the \textit{fast time} signal. The fast time signal is sampled with a sampling rate $f_{s, FT}$

The pressure wave emitted by the heart mostly remains unchanged in large and mid-size arteries, like the radial artery. Only a drop in mean pressure of a few $\si{\mmHg}$ can be observed, due to viscous effects. 

The peak value of the pressure, measured at any location, is called the \textit{systolic pressure} $p_s$. The minimal pressure value is denoted the \textit{diastolic pressure} $p_d$. The difference between the systolic and diastolic pressures is known as the \textit{pulse pressure} $p_{pulse} = p_s - p_d$. Blood pressure is traditionally expressed in $\si{\mmHg}$ ($\SI{1}{\mmHg} \approx \SI{133.322}{\pascal}$).

\textit{Compliance} describes the change in vessel dimensions in response to a change in internal pressure. \textcite{hellevik_cardiovascular} defines compliance as a local property of the vessel wall and expresses it in terms of the cross-sectional area $A$ and the internal pressure $p$ as:

\begin{equation}
C = \frac{\partial A}{\partial p}.
\end{equation}

This represents the change in cross-sectional area per unit change in pressure and can be interpreted as a local measure of vessel compliance. A related quantity is the \textit{distensibility} $D$, which is the compliance normalized by the cross-sectional area,

\begin{equation}
D = \frac{1}{A}\frac{\partial A}{\partial p}
= \frac{C}{A}.
\end{equation}

Blood vessels with a wall thickness $h$ much smaller than the vessel radius $r$, such that $h \ll r$, are referred to as \textit{thin-walled vessels}. For thin-walled vessels, the compliance can be approximated as:
\begin{equation}
C \approx 2 \frac{r}{h} \frac{A}{\eta},
\end{equation}
